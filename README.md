# Multi-Vendor Coordination Playbook

> Field-based approaches to sourcing, negotiating, coordinating, and evaluating vendors across large-scale brand activation campaigns, trade shows, and promotional events.

---

## Overview

Managing multiple vendors simultaneously is one of the most operationally demanding aspects of large-scale event and campaign delivery. A single campaign can involve suppliers across audio-visual, catering, transport, printing, fabrication, staffing, and venue services — each with their own timelines, costs, and quality standards.

This playbook documents practical approaches developed across agency and event production environments, where vendor coordination directly determined whether campaigns were delivered on time, on budget, and to standard.

These are working methods, not theoretical frameworks.

---

## 1. Vendor Sourcing & Selection

### The Core Challenge
For large campaigns, finding the right vendor is not just about price — it is about reliability, capacity, and fit for the specific operational context. A vendor who performs well for a small activation may not have the resources to deliver at scale.

### Approach

**Build and maintain a vendor pool**
Rather than sourcing from scratch for every campaign, a working vendor list is maintained across service categories. Each entry includes contact details, service scope, past performance notes, and pricing benchmarks. This reduces sourcing time significantly and allows for faster mobilisation when campaign timelines are tight.

**Define requirements before approaching vendors**
Before any vendor is contacted, the campaign brief is translated into a clear operational requirements document — covering scope of work, quantities, delivery schedule, site conditions, and any compliance requirements. Vendors are assessed against these requirements, not just against price.

**Shortlist and compare**
For significant service categories, a minimum of three vendor quotes are obtained and compared across price, capacity, lead time, and track record. For repeat vendors with a strong performance history, this step may be streamlined — but is never skipped entirely for new or high-risk service categories.

**Assess operational fit**
A vendor's ability to work within the campaign's logistical constraints — site access, time windows, equipment requirements, inter-vendor dependencies — is evaluated alongside their core service capability. Operational incompatibility causes more campaign disruptions than poor quality.

## 2. Negotiation Approaches

### The Core Challenge
Campaign budgets are fixed. Vendor costs, however, are rarely fixed at first quote. Effective negotiation protects margin without damaging the supplier relationships that campaigns depend on.

### Approach

**Negotiate on value, not just price**
The most productive negotiations focus on the overall value of the engagement — payment terms, bundled services, priority scheduling, and long-term volume commitments — rather than purely driving down the unit price. Vendors who feel fairly treated deliver better and respond faster when problems arise.

**Use volume and repeat business as leverage**
Where a vendor relationship spans multiple campaigns, the cumulative value of that relationship is used as a negotiating anchor. Preferred vendor status — with the reliability and forward visibility it offers — is a meaningful incentive for vendors to offer competitive terms.

**Be clear on non-negotiables early**
Timeline, quality standards, and compliance requirements are stated clearly at the outset of any negotiation. These are not negotiating variables. This prevents misalignment later and ensures vendors self-select out if they cannot meet core requirements.

**Document everything agreed**
All agreed terms — pricing, scope, delivery schedule, cancellation conditions — are confirmed in writing before any work commences. Verbal agreements are a liability in multi-vendor environments where dependencies are high.

## 3. On-Ground Vendor Coordination

### The Core Challenge
On the day of a campaign or event, vendors arrive at different times, require different site access, and depend on each other in ways that are not always visible until something goes wrong. Coordination at this stage is real-time problem solving.

### Approach

**Develop a master vendor schedule**
Before campaign day, a consolidated schedule is prepared covering every vendor's arrival time, setup window, site access requirements, point of contact, and dependencies on other vendors. This single document is shared with all relevant team members and serves as the operational anchor on the day.

**Assign a single point of contact per vendor**
Each vendor has one designated contact from the operations team. This prevents conflicting instructions, reduces confusion, and ensures accountability on both sides.

**Conduct a pre-event briefing**
Where possible, all key vendors are briefed together before setup begins — covering the day's schedule, site layout, inter-vendor dependencies, and escalation contacts. This surfaces potential conflicts early and aligns expectations before they become problems.

**Anticipate dependency risks**
In multi-vendor environments, one delay cascades. The vendor schedule is mapped for dependencies — for example, fabrication must be complete before audio-visual setup can begin, which must be complete before catering access is possible. Critical path items are monitored actively throughout the day.

**Stay mobile and visible**
During campaign execution, the coordination role is not desk-based. Active presence across vendor workstreams allows problems to be identified and resolved before they escalate. Vendors perform better when they know someone is watching and can make decisions on the spot.

## 4. Post-Event Vendor Evaluation

### The Core Challenge
Without a structured review process, the same vendor problems repeat across campaigns. Post-event evaluation closes the loop — it turns operational experience into institutional knowledge.

### Approach

**Evaluate against agreed scope**
Each vendor is assessed against the specific deliverables and standards agreed at the outset. Assessment is factual and documented — not based on general impressions.

**Record performance notes immediately**
Evaluation is most accurate when completed within 48 hours of campaign close, while details are fresh. Notes cover what was delivered, what fell short, how problems were handled, and whether the vendor communicated proactively when issues arose.

**Update the vendor pool**
Performance notes are added to the working vendor list. Strong performers are flagged as preferred vendors for future campaigns. Underperforming vendors are flagged with specific notes — not necessarily removed, but approached with adjusted expectations or additional oversight in future engagements.

**Feed learnings into future briefs**
Recurring issues with specific service categories — late delivery, underquoting, site access problems — are used to refine the requirements documents and vendor briefings used in future campaigns. The goal is for each campaign to be operationally smoother than the last.

## Key Learnings

- **The vendor relationship is an operational asset.** Vendors who trust you prioritise your campaigns when capacity is tight. This matters most when timelines compress.
- **Most on-day problems are pre-day failures.** Poor briefing, unclear scope, and unconfirmed logistics cause the majority of execution-day disruptions.
- **Cheap vendors are rarely cheap.** The cost of a vendor failure — in time, rework, and client impact — almost always exceeds the saving made at negotiation.
- **Documentation is coordination infrastructure.** In a multi-vendor environment, verbal communication breaks down under pressure. Written schedules, confirmed scopes, and shared briefing documents are what hold the operation together.
